## Real-World Graphs

To evaluate the systems under more realistic conditions, we extended our benchmarking to synthetic graph topologies that approximate the structure of real-world networks. Specifically, we tested on directed Barabási-Albert (BA) graphs and generic scale-free networks. These models effectively capture the power-law degree distributions commonly found in social networks, biological systems, and the Web [BA99]. They are characterized by a preferential attachment structure, which results in a few highly connected "hub" nodes and remarkably short average path lengths.

The performance trends on these complex topologies largely align with our previous observations across the different recursion variants, but the unique structure of scale-free networks deeply exposes the architectural bottlenecks of several database engines. XSB, for instance, exhibited a notable performance dichotomy. While its subsumptive tabling mechanism allowed it to gracefully navigate the density of Barabási-Albert graphs—successfully computing closures for up to 100,000 nodes in just a few seconds—it surprisingly got trapped during evaluation on generic scale-free networks. On these graphs, XSB became completely unresponsive and had to be manually aborted, revealing an unforeseen limitation of its tabling strategy when confronted with specific power-law structures.

Conversely, traditional relational databases (PostgreSQL, MariaDB, and CockroachDB) struggled significantly. Although their ability to spill intermediate results to disk prevents them from crashing during memory-intensive operations, this resilience comes at a severe performance cost. For networks larger than 60,000 nodes, execution times stretched beyond 12 minutes, rendering these systems practically unviable for large-scale, highly connected graphs.

DuckDB presented an interesting contrast. Its vectorized analytical engine delivered impressive execution speeds—often orders of magnitude faster than the traditional SQL systems—but its strict reliance on in-memory materialization proved to be a fatal flaw. For graph sizes of 90,000 nodes and above, the massive volume of intermediate data generated by recursive joins on dense networks caused DuckDB to consistently crash with Out-of-Memory (OOM) errors.

Graph-native systems like Neo4j faced similar roadblocks. Neo4j evaluates queries by enumerating all possible variable-length paths before applying deduplication. In a scale-free network, the combination of short path lengths and high-degree hubs creates an exponential explosion of possible traversals. As a result, Neo4j rapidly exhausted its memory pool, hitting OOM errors on graphs as small as 30,000 nodes.

Ultimately, we observe a clear architectural divide when dealing with dense, real-world graphs: engines that rely on aggressive in-memory materialization or exhaustive path enumeration quickly hit a hard memory wall, while systems that prioritize memory resilience suffer from unacceptable execution delays.
